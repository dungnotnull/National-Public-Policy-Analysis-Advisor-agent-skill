# Stakeholder Analysis Framework

## Framework Overview

Stakeholder analysis identifies who affects, is affected by, or has an interest in a policy issue, and maps their positions, power, and interests. This framework operationalizes Sabatier & Jenkins-Smith's Advocacy Coalition Framework and Freeman's network analysis methodology.

## Core Principles

### 1. Stakeholders Are Political Actors

Stakeholders are not passive groups—they actively shape policy through advocacy, coalition-building, and resource mobilization. Understanding their interests and capabilities is essential to policy feasibility.

### 2. Stakeholders Have Multiple Attributes

Each stakeholder has:
- **Interests:** What they want from policy outcomes
- **Positions:** What they publicly advocate for
- **Power:** Their ability to influence outcomes
- **Perspectives:** How they understand the problem

### 3. Coalitions Form Around Shared Beliefs

Stakeholders form advocacy coalitions based on shared core beliefs rather than narrow interests. These coalitions are relatively stable over time.

## Step-by-Step Methodology

### Step 1: Identify Stakeholder Categories

Brainstorm all potential stakeholders across these categories:

```
Government actors:
- Executive branch (ministries, agencies)
- Legislative (committees, caucuses)
- Sub-national (state/provincial, local)

Private sector:
- Industry associations
- Individual firms
- Labor unions
- Professional associations

Civil society:
- Advocacy groups
- NGOs and charities
- Social movements
- Religious organizations

International actors:
- Foreign governments
- International organizations
- Multi-national corporations

Individual citizens:
- Affected populations
- General public
- Future generations
```

### Step 2: Map Stakeholder Attributes

For each identified stakeholder, assess:

**Interests:**
```
Material interests: Economic gains/losses
Ideational interests: Values, beliefs, principles
Political interests: Power, influence, legitimacy
Institutional interests: Agency survival, budget, authority
```

**Positions:**
```
Public stance: What they advocate for publicly
Private stance: What they actually prefer
Flexibility: How firm are these positions
Negotiability: What can be traded off
```

**Power Resources:**
```
Formal authority: Legal/political power
Economic resources: Money, market power
Social capital: Networks, relationships
Information: Expertise, data access
Moral authority: Legitimacy, credibility
Mobilization capacity: Ability to activate supporters
```

**Likelihood of Engagement:**
```
High: Strong interest + high power
Medium: Strong interest OR high power
Low: Weak interest AND low power
```

### Step 3: Analyze Stakeholder Positions

Map stakeholders along support/opposition dimensions:

```
Strong Support ↔ Neutral ↔ Strong Opposition
```

For each stakeholder:
- What is their position on key policy dimensions?
- How intense is their support/opposition?
- What are the "red lines" they won't cross?
- Where is there potential for compromise?

### Step 4: Identify Advocacy Coalitions

Group stakeholders into coalitions based on:

```
Shared core beliefs: Fundamental views about the problem
Shared policy preferences: Similar desired solutions
Coordinate actions: Joint advocacy, resource sharing
Stable over time: Endure beyond specific issues
```

For each coalition:
- Who belongs to this coalition?
- What core beliefs unify them?
- What resources do they collectively control?
- What strategies do they employ?

### Step 5: Map Conflict Patterns

Analyze the structure of conflict:

```
Primary conflict lines: Where are the main cleavages?
Secondary conflicts: What other divisions exist?
Potential alliances: Who might collaborate on specific aspects?
Bridge actors: Who can span between coalitions?
```

### Step 6: Assess Influence Pathways

Identify how stakeholders exert influence:

```
Formal channels: Legislative process, court cases, regulatory proceedings
Informal channels: Lobbying, media campaigns, public opinion
Direct action: Protests, strikes, boycotts
Expertise: Research, policy analysis, technical advice
Convening power: Ability to bring others together
```

## Analysis Frameworks

### Power-Interest Grid

Map stakeholders on a 2x2 grid:

```
        | Low Interest | High Interest
--------|---------------|---------------
High    |   Keep       |   Key Players
Power   |  Satisfied   |   (Manage Closely)
--------|---------------|---------------
Low     |   Monitor    |   Keep
Power   |   (Minimal)  |  Informed
```

**Implications:**
- **Key Players:** High power, high interest → Primary focus of engagement
- **Keep Satisfied:** High power, low interest → Don't arouse opposition
- **Keep Informed:** Low power, high interest → Ensure their voices are heard
- **Monitor:** Low power, low interest → Minimal attention needed

### Position-Intensity Matrix

Map stakeholders by position and intensity:

```
                | Strong Opposition | Moderate | Neutral | Moderate | Strong Support
----------------|-------------------|----------|---------|----------|---------------
Low Intensity   |                   |          |         |          |
Medium Intensity|                   |          |         |          |
High Intensity  |   Veto Players    |          |         |          | Champions
```

**Implications:**
- **Veto Players:** Strong opposition + high intensity → Can block policy
- **Champions:** Strong support + high intensity → Can drive policy forward
- **Movable Middle:** Moderate positions + medium intensity → Potential for persuasion

## Quality Checks

Good stakeholder analysis should:

```
✓ Identify all relevant stakeholders (not just obvious ones)
✓ Distinguish between positions and underlying interests
✓ Assess power resources, not just formal status
✓ Recognize coalition structures and conflict lines
✓ Identify potential for compromise and alliance-building
✓ Consider future generations and absent stakeholders
```

**Red flags:**
```
✗ Focuses only on government actors
✗ Assumes positions reveal underlying interests
✗ Treats all stakeholders as equally powerful
✗ Assumes stakeholders are unitary (may have internal divisions)
✗ Neglects international or cross-jurisdictional actors
✗ Forgets affected populations who lack formal voice
```

## Application Examples

### Example 1: Carbon Pricing Policy

**Stakeholder Categories Identified:**

**Government:**
- Finance ministry (revenue interest, carbon pricing supportive)
- Environment ministry (climate interest, carbon pricing supportive)
- Industry ministry (competitiveness concern, carbon pricing wary)
- Opposition parties (varied positions, interest in political advantage)

**Private Sector:**
- Heavy industry associations (competitiveness concern, oppose)
- Clean technology firms (market opportunity, support)
- Energy companies (varied by portfolio, split)
- Labor unions (job concerns, mixed)

**Civil Society:**
- Environmental NGOs (climate concern, strong support)
- Business associations (competitiveness concern, oppose)
- Consumer groups (cost concern, wary)
- Youth climate movement (intergenerational justice, strong support)

**Advocacy Coalitions:**

1. **Climate Action Coalition:**
   - Environmental NGOs, youth climate movement, clean tech
   - Core belief: Urgent climate action requires carbon pricing
   - High mobilization capacity, moral authority

2. **Competitiveness Coalition:**
   - Heavy industry, business associations, energy sector
   - Core belief: Carbon pricing harms economic competitiveness
   - High economic resources, political access

3. **Revenue-Neutral Coalition:**
   - Some consumer groups, fiscal conservatives, some unions
   - Core belief: Carbon pricing acceptable if revenue recycled
   - Moderate intensity, potential bridge between coalitions

**Conflict Pattern:**
- Primary cleavage: Climate action vs. economic competitiveness
- Secondary cleavage: Revenue use (rebates vs. spending vs. tax cuts)
- Bridge actors: Finance ministry, centrist parties, moderate business groups

### Example 2: Housing Affordability

**Stakeholder Categories:**

**Government:**
- Housing ministry (affordability focus, various solutions)
- Finance ministry (market stability focus, market-oriented)
- Local governments (zoning authority, NIMBY pressures)
- Central bank (financial stability, market concerns)

**Private Sector:**
- Real estate developers (profit interest, supply-side solutions)
- Construction industry (job interest, supportive of development)
- Financial institutions (mortgage lending, market stability)
- Landlords (rental income, tenant regulations opposition)
- Real estate agents (transaction volume, market activity)

**Civil Society:**
- Tenants' rights groups (renter protections, strong advocacy)
- Homeowners' associations (property values, development opposition)
- Housing NGOs (affordability focus, supportive of solutions)
- Social justice groups (inequality focus, rights-based approach)
- YIMBY groups (pro-development, supply focus)

**Advocacy Coalitions:**

1. **Supply-Side Coalition:**
   - Developers, construction, YIMBY groups, some economists
   - Core belief: Build more housing to solve affordability
   - High economic resources, fragmented political power

2. **Tenant Protection Coalition:**
   - Tenants' rights groups, social justice groups, some unions
   - Core belief: Protect tenants from market forces
   - High mobilization capacity, moral authority

3. **Property Values Defense Coalition:**
   - Homeowners, real estate agents, some local governments
   - Core belief: Protect neighborhood character and property values
   - High local political power, strong local presence

**Conflict Pattern:**
- Primary cleavage: Supply solutions vs. tenant protections
- Secondary cleavage: Development vs. neighborhood preservation
- Bridge actors: Moderate housing groups, centrist politicians

## Common Pitfalls

### 1. Treating Stakeholders as Unitary

Many organizations have internal divisions. Businesses may have different departments with different interests. Government agencies may have bureaucratic factions. Always ask: "Does this stakeholder have internal divisions?"

### 2. Confusing Positions with Interests

What stakeholders say they want (positions) may differ from what they actually want (interests). Positions are negotiating positions; interests are underlying motivations. Always probe: "What does this stakeholder really care about?"

### 3. Overlooking Invisible Stakeholders

Future generations, non-human species, dispersed beneficiaries, and marginalized groups often lack formal voice but have legitimate interests. Explicitly include them in analysis.

### 4. Static Analysis

Stakeholders change positions, form new alliances, gain or lose power. Treat stakeholder analysis as dynamic, not static. Update as context changes.
