# Evidence Synthesis for Policy Analysis

## Framework Overview

Evidence synthesis systematically identifies, evaluates, and integrates findings from multiple sources to inform policy decisions. This framework operationalizes systematic review methodology adapted for policy analysis contexts.

## Core Principles

### 1. Evidence Informs, It Doesn't Decide

Evidence is one input into policy decisions, alongside values, politics, feasibility, and judgment. Evidence can't "speak for itself"—it requires interpretation in context.

### 2. All Evidence Is Not Equal

Evidence quality varies enormously. Randomized controlled trials are more reliable than anecdotes. Peer-reviewed studies are more reliable than unpublished reports. Explicitly assess evidence quality.

### 3. Synthesis Requires Structure

Systematic evidence synthesis follows a structured process to minimize bias and maximize comprehensiveness. Haphazard literature review risks cherry-picking and confirmation bias.

### 4. Gaps and Limitations Matter

Knowing what we don't know is as important as knowing what we do know. Explicitly identify evidence gaps and limitations in the knowledge base.

## Step-by-Step Methodology

### Step 1: Define the Evidence Question

Translate policy questions into answerable evidence questions:

```
Population: Who is affected? (e.g., "adults with incomes below $30k")
Intervention: What policy or program? (e.g., "earned income tax credits")
Comparison: Compared to what? (e.g., "no tax credit program")
Outcome: What matters? (e.g., "employment rates, earnings, child outcomes")
Study design: What evidence types? (e.g., "experimental or quasi-experimental")
```

**Template PICO:**
```
In [Population], does [Intervention] compared to [Comparison] affect [Outcomes]?
```

### Step 2: Search for Evidence

Use systematic search strategies:

**Search sources:**
```
Academic databases: PubMed, EconLit, Web of Science, Google Scholar
Policy databases: OECD, World Bank, government policy repositories
Gray literature: Think tank reports, government evaluations, NGO studies
Expert consultation: Subject matter experts, policy practitioners
```

**Search strategy:**
```
Keywords: Core concepts and synonyms
Boolean operators: AND, OR, NOT
Snowballing: Forward and backward citation tracking
Time bounds: Usually last 10-15 years unless seminal older work
```

**Documentation:**
Document search strategy for reproducibility:
- Databases searched
- Search terms used
- Inclusion/exclusion criteria
- Number of results at each stage

### Step 3: Assess Evidence Quality

Evaluate each study using appropriate criteria:

**For impact evaluations:**
```
Internal validity: Causal attribution (RCT > quasi-experimental > observational)
External validity: Generalizability to other contexts
Measurement quality: Outcome measurement validity and reliability
Sample size: Statistical power and precision
Publication status: Peer-reviewed > unpublished
```

**For qualitative evidence:**
```
Theoretical grounding: Connection to established theory
Methodological rigor: Data collection and analysis methods
Triangulation: Multiple sources or methods
Credibility: Confidence in findings
Transferability: Applicability to other contexts
```

**For gray literature:**
```
Author credibility: Organization or author expertise
Methodological transparency: Methods clearly described
Data sources: Primary or secondary data sources
Peer review: Internal or external review process
Funding transparency: Conflicts of interest disclosed
```

**Quality classification:**
```
High: RCTs, well-designed quasi-experiments, rigorous systematic reviews
Medium: Observational studies with controls, good quasi-experiments
Low: Observational without controls, case studies, expert opinion
Very Low: Anecdotes, opinion pieces, methodologically weak studies
```

### Step 4: Extract Findings

Systematically extract key information:

```
Study design and methods
Sample characteristics
Context details (country, time, setting)
Intervention details
Outcomes measured
Results (effect sizes, statistical significance)
Limitations noted by authors
Funding sources and conflicts
```

**Standardized extraction:**
Use consistent format for easy comparison:
- Effect sizes with confidence intervals
- Statistical significance levels
- Subgroup analyses
- Contextual factors

### Step 5: Synthesize Across Studies

Look for patterns across studies:

```
Consistency: Do studies find similar effects?
Direction: Do effects point in same direction?
Magnitude: Are effect sizes similar?
Heterogeneity: What explains differences?
Contextual factors: What conditions moderate effects?
```

**Synthesis methods:**
```
Narrative synthesis: Qualitative integration of findings
Meta-analysis: Statistical combination of effect sizes
Subgroup analysis: Effects differ by population/context
Sensitivity analysis: Robustness to inclusion/exclusion decisions
```

### Step 6: Assess Strength of Evidence

Make overall judgment on evidence body:

```
Strong: Consistent findings from high-quality studies
Moderate: Generally consistent findings, some limitations
Limited: Inconsistent findings or significant limitations
Insufficient: Few studies or major evidence gaps
```

**Evidence grading framework:**
```
Grade A: High confidence in effect estimate
Grade B: Moderate confidence in effect estimate
Grade C: Low confidence in effect estimate
Grade D: Very low confidence in effect estimate
Grade I: Insufficient evidence to judge
```

### Step 7: Identify Evidence Gaps

Systematically document what we don't know:

```
Population gaps: Which groups understudied?
Intervention gaps: Which policy variations not evaluated?
Outcome gaps: Which important outcomes not measured?
Context gaps: Which contexts not represented?
Method gaps: What study types missing?
```

**Gap prioritization:**
```
Policy relevance: Does gap affect key decisions?
Actionability: Could evidence be generated?
Cost: What would it cost to fill gap?
Time: How long would filling gap take?
```

### Step 8: Translate for Policy Use

Make evidence accessible to policy audience:

```
Executive summary: Key findings in 2-3 paragraphs
Policy implications: What findings mean for decisions
Confidence assessment: How certain are we?
Practical guidance: What should policymakers do?
Evidence needs: What further research is needed?
```

## Quality Assurance

### Bias Prevention

**Search bias:**
```
Use multiple databases
Include gray literature
Search in multiple languages
Document search decisions
```

**Selection bias:**
```
Pre-specify inclusion/exclusion criteria
Apply criteria consistently
Document inclusion/exclusion decisions
Use multiple reviewers for selection
```

**Extraction bias:**
```
Use standardized extraction forms
Extract data independently by multiple reviewers
Resolve discrepancies through discussion
Document extraction decisions
```

**Interpretation bias:**
```
Consider all findings, not just significant results
Look for publication bias
Consider alternative explanations
Invite challenge from multiple perspectives
```

### Transparency

Make the synthesis process transparent:

```
Document every step of the process
Make search strategy reproducible
Report all studies considered, not just included
Disclose limitations and uncertainties
Acknowledge reviewer biases
```

## Application Example: Minimum Wage Impacts

### Evidence Question

"In advanced economies, does increasing minimum wage compared to no increase affect employment, wages, and poverty?"

### Evidence Sources

**Academic studies:**
- Card & Krueger (1994): Natural experiment comparing fast-food employment
- Dube et al. (2010): Border county analysis
- Cengiz et al. (2019): Event study methodology
- Meta-analyses: Doucouliagos & Stanley (2009), Belman & Wolfson (2014)

**Government evaluations:**
- UK Low Pay Commission evaluations
- US Congressional Budget Office analyses

**Think tank reports:**
- Economic Policy Institute
- Employment Policies Institute

### Evidence Quality Assessment

**High quality:**
- Recent quasi-experimental studies using border discontinuities
- Event study methodologies with strong controls
- Meta-analyses of RCTs and quasi-experiments

**Medium quality:**
- Observational studies with controls
- Government evaluations with robust methods

**Low quality:**
- Simple before-after comparisons
- Studies with weak controls
- Advocacy organization reports with cherry-picked evidence

### Synthesis Findings

**Employment effects:**
- Consensus: Minimal to no negative employment effects for moderate increases
- Heterogeneity: Larger effects possible for very large increases or specific sectors
- Evidence strength: Moderate to strong (consistent across high-quality studies)
- Context matters: Effects vary by economic conditions, region, industry

**Wage effects:**
- Consensus: Clear positive wage effects for low-wage workers
- Evidence strength: Strong (consistent across all studies)
- Magnitude: Wage increases proportional to minimum wage increase

**Poverty effects:**
- Consensus: Moderate poverty reduction, but not all low-wage workers are in poor households
- Evidence strength: Moderate (fewer studies, more heterogeneity)
- Limitations: Poverty effects depend on household structure, spillover effects

**Distributional effects:**
- Findings: Benefits concentrated among low-wage workers, but some job loss possible
- Evidence strength: Moderate (incomplete evidence on distributional consequences)
- Policy implication: Complementary policies needed for affected workers

### Evidence Gaps

**Population gaps:**
- Developing country evidence limited
- Youth and specific demographics understudied
- Long-term career effects not well studied

**Intervention gaps:**
- Very large minimum wage increases (>$15) not yet evaluated
- Interaction with other labor market policies not well studied
- Regional variations within countries not well understood

**Outcome gaps:**
- Non-employment outcomes (health, education, crime) rarely studied
- Employer adjustment strategies not well documented
- Informal sector effects not well understood

### Policy Implications

**Confidence level:**
- High confidence: Wage effects positive
- Moderate confidence: Employment effects minimal for moderate increases
- Moderate confidence: Poverty effects positive but incomplete
- Low confidence: Long-term and economy-wide effects

**Practical guidance:**
- Moderate minimum wage increases likely positive net effect
- Very large increases require caution due to employment uncertainty
- Regional differentiation can mitigate negative employment effects
- Complementary policies (EITC, training) can enhance poverty impacts

**Evidence needs:**
- More evaluation of very large increases
- Better data on long-term career effects
- More evidence from developing countries
- Studies of interaction with other policies

## Common Pitfalls

### 1. Cherry-Picking

Selecting only studies that support preferred conclusions. Solution: Systematic search and transparent inclusion criteria.

### 2. Over-Generalization

Applying findings beyond contexts where evidence was generated. Solution: Explicit attention to context and external validity.

### 3. Confusing "No Evidence" with "Evidence of No Effect"

Absence of evidence doesn't prove absence of effect. Solution: Explicitly discuss evidence gaps.

### 4. Ignoring Study Quality

Treating all studies as equally valid. Solution: Explicit quality assessment and weighting.

### 5. Publication Bias

Published studies more likely to show positive effects. Solution: Look for unpublished literature, assess publication bias.

## Advanced Techniques

### Network Meta-Analysis

When comparing multiple interventions not directly compared:

```
Indirect comparisons through common comparators
Rankings of intervention effectiveness
Handling of complex evidence networks
```

### Realist Synthesis

For understanding what works for whom in what contexts:

```
Focus on mechanisms and contexts
Theory-driven approach
Explanation building, not just effect estimation
```

### Rapid Evidence Assessment

When time is limited:

```
Focused search on key sources
Quality assessment streamlined
Limited synthesis but transparent about limitations
```
