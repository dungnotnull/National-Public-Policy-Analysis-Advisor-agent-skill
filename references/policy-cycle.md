# Policy Cycle Framework

## Framework Overview

The policy cycle provides a structured way to understand the stages through which policies develop, are implemented, and are evaluated. This framework operationalizes Lasswell's Decision Process model and Jann & Wegrich's modern policy cycle framework.

## Core Principles

### 1. Policy Is a Process, Not an Event

Policies evolve through stages over time. Understanding which stage a policy is in helps identify constraints, opportunities, and appropriate analytical approaches.

### 2. Stages Are Interconnected

Each stage influences and is influenced by other stages. Implementation problems can lead to agenda re-setting. Evaluation findings can trigger new formulation.

### 3. The Cycle Is Not Linear

Real policy processes don't neatly progress through stages in order. Policies can cycle back, skip stages, or have multiple stages occurring simultaneously.

### 4. Multiple Streams Interact

Policy progress requires three streams to converge (Kingdon's Multiple Streams framework):
- **Problem stream:** Problems get attention
- **Policy stream:** Solutions are available
- **Politics stream:** Political will exists

## The Policy Cycle Stages

### Stage 1: Agenda Setting

**Definition:** Which issues get government attention and why.

**Key Questions:**
```
How did this issue get on the agenda?
Why now rather than earlier or later?
Who are the agenda setters?
What competing issues were pushed aside?
What role did events/crises play?
```

**Agenda Types:**
```
Systemic agenda: Issues society thinks government should address
Institutional agenda: Issues government is actively considering
Decision agenda: Issues up for immediate decision
```

**Agenda Dynamics:**
- **Focusing events:** Crises, disasters, scandals that draw attention
- **Indicators:** Statistical measures that show problems
- **Feedback:** Information from existing policies
- **Advocacy:** Organized efforts to promote issues

**Analysis Focus:**
- What changed to put this issue on the agenda?
- How stable is agenda status? (Could it slip off?)
- What are the competing agenda items?
- Who benefits/loses from agenda status?

### Stage 2: Policy Formulation

**Definition:** Development of policy proposals and alternatives.

**Key Questions:**
```
Who is involved in formulating proposals?
What alternatives are being considered?
How are proposals being developed?
What evidence is being used?
What constraints shape formulation?
```

**Formulation Actors:**
```
Policy communities: Experts, bureaucrats, interest groups
Policy entrepreneurs: Champions for specific proposals
Knowledge brokers: Those who translate research for policy
Bureaucracies: Government agencies with expertise
```

**Formulation Process:**
```
Problem definition: How is the issue framed?
Alternative generation: What solutions are considered?
Analysis: What evidence and analysis informs proposals?
Consultation: Who is consulted in development?
Drafting: How are proposals put into formal form?
```

**Analysis Focus:**
- What formulation process is being used?
- How inclusive/exclusive is the process?
- What evidence base informs proposals?
- What political constraints shape options?
- What technical constraints shape feasibility?

### Stage 3: Decision Making

**Definition:** Formal adoption of policy choices.

**Key Questions:**
```
Who makes the decision? (Legislature, executive, agency, court?)
What decision rule applies? (Majority, supermajority, executive order?)
What are the decision criteria?
What tradeoffs are being made?
What is the likelihood of adoption?
```

**Decision Venues:**
```
Legislative: Laws passed by parliament/congress
Executive: Orders, decrees, regulations from executives
Administrative: Rulemaking by agencies
Judicial: Court decisions creating policy
Direct: Referenda, initiatives, citizen assemblies
```

**Decision Criteria:**
```
Political viability: Can it pass? Will it be sustained?
Technical feasibility: Can it be implemented?
Economic efficiency: Does it pass cost-benefit?
Public support: Is there public backing?
Stakeholder support: Do key stakeholders support it?
```

**Analysis Focus:**
- What decision venue is being used?
- What coalition is needed for adoption?
- What are the political constraints?
- What compromises are needed?
- What is the timeline for decision?

### Stage 4: Implementation

**Definition:** Putting policy into practice.

**Key Questions:**
```
Who is responsible for implementation?
What resources are available?
What discretion do implementers have?
What are the implementation challenges?
How is implementation monitored?
```

**Implementation Gap:**
```
Policy design → Policy outcomes
        ↕
    Implementation
(The gap between design and outcomes)
```

**Implementation Challenges:**
```
Resource constraints: Insufficient funding, staff, expertise
Discretion: Implementers interpret and adapt policy
Coordination: Multiple agencies need to work together
Target group behavior: Those affected may not comply
Politics: Ongoing political opposition or support
Complexity: Policy problem may be more complex than anticipated
```

**Implementation Styles:**
```
Top-down: Clear hierarchical control from policymakers
Bottom-up: Street-level bureaucrats shape implementation
Interactive: Negotiated between levels and actors
```

**Analysis Focus:**
- What implementation challenges are likely?
- What resources are available?
- How much discretion do implementers have?
- What political dynamics affect implementation?
- What monitoring/evaluation is planned?

### Stage 5: Evaluation

**Definition:** Assessing policy effects and processes.

**Key Questions:**
```
What is being evaluated? (Outcomes, processes, costs?)
How are effects being measured?
What comparison is being made?
What evaluation methods are used?
How are findings being used?
```

**Evaluation Types:**
```
Process evaluation: How was policy implemented?
Outcome evaluation: What effects did policy have?
Impact evaluation: What causal effects can be attributed?
Cost-effectiveness: What are costs per unit of outcome?
Cost-benefit: Do benefits exceed costs?
```

**Evaluation Methods:**
```
Experimental: Randomized controlled trials
Quasi-experimental: Difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity
Non-experimental: Before-after, cross-sectional
Qualitative: Case studies, interviews, observation
Mixed methods: Combining quantitative and qualitative
```

**Evaluation Use:**
```
Instrumental use: Direct application to decisions
Conceptual use: Influencing thinking and understanding
Political use: Justifying predetermined positions
Symbolic use: Demonstrating accountability without real influence
```

**Analysis Focus:**
- What evaluation is planned/underway?
- What methods are being used?
- How rigorous is the evaluation?
- What are the evaluation limitations?
- How might findings be used?

## Stage Dynamics

### Feedback Loops

```
Evaluation findings → Agenda setting (new issues identified)
Implementation problems → Formulation (policy redesign)
Political changes → Decision making (reconsideration)
```

### Non-Linearity

```
Skipped stages: Emergency actions may skip formulation
Simultaneous stages: Implementation may begin before full decision
Reversals: Evaluation may trigger return to formulation
Parallel tracks: Multiple issues progressing through different stages
```

### Multiple Levels

```
Policy cycles at different levels:
- International (e.g., climate agreements)
- National (e.g., federal legislation)
- Sub-national (e.g., state/provincial policies)
- Local (e.g., municipal ordinances)
```

## Contextual Factors

### Political System Effects

```
Majoritarian systems: Faster agenda changes, less stability
Consensus systems: Slower agenda changes, more stability
Presidential systems: Separation of powers, veto points
Parliamentary systems: Fused powers, easier passage
```

### Institutional Factors

```
Federal vs. unitary: Multiple venues vs. single venue
Strong vs. weak legislatures: Legislative vs. executive dominance
Independent agencies: Technocratic insulation vs. democratic control
Judicial review: Court involvement in policy
```

### External Factors

```
Economic conditions: Growth enables policy, recession constrains
International influences: Cross-national learning, international agreements
Technological change: Creates new problems and solutions
Demographic change: Shifts problem definition and coalition composition
```

## Application Example: Carbon Pricing Policy

### Agenda Setting Stage

**How it got on agenda:**
- Climate change became visible problem (extreme weather, scientific consensus)
- International commitments created pressure (Paris Agreement)
- Advocacy by environmental groups raised visibility
- Economic analysis showed carbon pricing as efficient solution

**Agenda dynamics:**
- Shifted from systemic to institutional agenda
- Competing with other environmental and economic priorities
- Focusing events: Climate disasters, international summits

**Analysis focus:**
- Carbon pricing is on institutional agenda but not decision agenda
- Climate advocates pushing for decision status
- Industry opposition attempting to keep off decision agenda
- Economic conditions (recession vs. growth) affect agenda priority

### Formulation Stage

**Formulation actors:**
- Finance ministry (revenue options, design details)
- Environment ministry (environmental effectiveness)
- Industry groups (competitiveness concerns)
- Environmental NGOs (environmental ambition)
- Academic experts (optimal design)

**Formulation process:**
- Multiple policy documents produced
- Stakeholder consultations held
- Expert reports commissioned
- International examples studied

**Alternatives considered:**
- Carbon tax vs. cap-and-trade vs. hybrid
- Economy-wide vs. sector-specific
- Revenue recycling options (rebates, tax cuts, spending)

**Analysis focus:**
- Carbon tax design being formulated (rate, coverage, exemptions)
- Revenue recycling still being debated
- Border adjustments under consideration
- Complementary policies being identified

### Decision Stage

**Decision venue:**
- Legislative approval needed for major design elements
- Executive authority for some implementation details
- Regulatory approval for detailed rules

**Decision criteria:**
- Environmental effectiveness: Will it reduce emissions?
- Economic efficiency: Is it cost-effective?
- Political viability: Can it pass legislature?
- Competitiveness: Will it harm business?
- Equity: Who bears costs/benefits?

**Coalition needed:**
- Environmental groups + business associations + government
- Right balance of revenue recycling to get support
- Regional considerations (have-not provinces)

**Analysis focus:**
- Legislative approval uncertain without industry support
- Revenue recycling is key to building coalition
- International competitiveness concerns must be addressed
- Timeline: 1-2 years for major legislation, longer if controversial

### Implementation Stage

**Implementation responsibility:**
- Tax authority for carbon tax administration
- Environment ministry for oversight
- Regulatory body for compliance and enforcement

**Implementation challenges:**
- Data collection on emissions (new reporting requirements)
- Compliance monitoring (need verification systems)
- Border adjustments (complex international coordination)
- Political pushback (ongoing opposition)

**Discretion:**
- Rulemaking creates interpretation opportunities
- Enforcement creates discretion in strictness
- Compliance assistance shapes how firms respond

**Analysis focus:**
- Administration capacity exists (tax authority experienced)
- Data systems will need development
- Border adjustments will be complex to implement
- Ongoing political opposition may undermine compliance

### Evaluation Stage

**Planned evaluation:**
- Emissions monitoring and reporting
- Economic impact assessment
- Revenue use evaluation
- Distributional impact assessment

**Evaluation methods:**
- Emissions: Measured and reported by firms
- Economic: Before-after analysis, modeling
- Distributional: Household surveys, tax data
- International: Comparative analysis

**Evaluation limitations:**
- Attribution problems (other policies affect emissions)
- Economic modeling uncertainty
- Distributional analysis complexity
- International comparison difficulties

**Analysis focus:**
- Evaluation will show emissions reductions or not
- Economic impacts will be contested (model limitations)
- Distributional impacts will be politically salient
- Evaluation may trigger agenda re-setting (adjust rate, expand coverage)

## Common Pitfalls

### 1. Assuming Linear Progression

Real policy processes rarely move neatly through stages. Look for parallel tracks, reversals, and skipped stages.

### 2. Ignoring Power Dynamics

Each stage involves power struggles. Who wins/loses at each stage? Who has veto power?

### 3. Forgetting International Context

Many policy issues operate across borders. International influences matter for agenda, formulation, and implementation.

### 4. Neglecting Street-Level Bureaucracy

Implementation often diverges from design due to front-line worker discretion and adaptation.

### 5. Assuming Evaluation Is Used Objectively

Evaluation findings are often used politically. Analyze how, not whether, evaluation is used.
